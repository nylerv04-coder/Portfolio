Testing Engineering Principles: L-Bracket

TYPE: Independent Project
DATE: November 2025
TOOLS: SolidWorks · Ansys · 3D Printer
SKILLS: CAD Modeling · FEA Simulation · Design Optimization
FIG. 1 — BASELINE L-BRACKET (LEFT) vs. OPTIMIZED L-BRACKET (RIGHT) — PRE-TEST

OBJECTIVE

After a few semesters of learning stress concentrations, FEA, and design-for-manufacturing in the classroom, I wanted to actually test the theory on a real part instead of trusting the textbook. I had also recently picked up a 3D printer, which made this the right time to close the loop between simulation and hardware on something small and self-contained.

The project runs through the full design cycle on two L-brackets with identical outer dimensions: a structurally naive baseline and an optimized version intended to carry significantly more load.

DESIGN APPROACH

FIG. 2 — SOLIDWORKS CAD: BASELINE L-BRACKET — SHARP 90° CORNER, NO GUSSET
FIG. 3 — SOLIDWORKS CAD: OPTIMIZED L-BRACKET — FILLET + TRIANGULAR GUSSET

Both brackets were modeled in SolidWorks. The baseline uses a sharp 90 degree internal corner, a classic stress concentration site where the notch effect funnels tensile stress to a single point and lowers the load required for crack initiation.

The optimized version addresses this with two changes: a fillet at the inner corner to spread stress over a larger area, and a triangular gusset on the inner face to stiffen the joint and shorten the bending moment arm. Print orientation was also adjusted so layer boundaries do not lie along the primary tensile load path, accounting for PLA's anisotropic behavior in FDM parts.

ENGINEERING DRAWINGS

Formal drawings were produced for both parts per standard drafting conventions. Both share the same outer dimensions: 1.75" mounting face width, 3.50" vertical height, 2.30" shelf depth, and 0.25" wall thickness. The only differences are the fillet and gusset on the optimized bracket.

FIG. 5 — ENGINEERING DRAWING: OPTIMIZED L-BRACKET (SCALE 1:2) — GUSSET + FILLET ANNOTATED

SIMULATION — ANSYS FEA

Both designs were run through static structural analysis in Ansys before printing, with a fixed boundary on the mounting face and a vertical load applied to the horizontal shelf. The von Mises stress plots matched the hypothesis: the baseline exceeded yield at the sharp corner, while the optimized design kept peak stresses within the safe range across the fillet and gusset.

FIG. 6 — ANSYS FEA: BASELINE L-BRACKET — PEAK VON MISES STRESS CONCENTRATED AT INNER CORNER
FIG. 7 — ANSYS FEA: OPTIMIZED L-BRACKET — STRESS DISTRIBUTED THROUGH GUSSET, NO CRITICAL CONCENTRATION

PHYSICAL TESTING

Both brackets were printed in PLA, mounted to a rigid cardboard surface, and loaded incrementally with a standard weight plate hung from the shelf. Loading continued until failure or until the target load was reached. Results matched the simulation predictions.

BRACKET DESIGN FEATURE FAILURE LOAD FAILURE MODE
Baseline L-Bracket Sharp 90° corner, no gusset < 10 kg Brittle fracture at inner corner
Optimized L-Bracket Fillet + triangular gusset > 10 kg (survived) None observed

The baseline fractured at the inner corner under 10 kg with no visible prior deformation, consistent with stress concentration-driven brittle failure in FDM PLA. The optimized bracket held the full 10 kg load with no fracture or visible deformation, confirming that the fillet and gusset redistributed stress away from the critical joint.

END OF EXPERIMENT

The baseline fractured cleanly at the inner corner, in the same location FEA had identified as the peak stress region. The optimized bracket, tested under the same conditions, showed no fracture or visible deformation.

FIG. 10 — POST-TEST: BROKEN BASELINE L-BRACKET (LEFT) vs. SURVIVING OPTIMIZED L-BRACKET (RIGHT)

TAKEAWAYS

Adding a fillet and gusset more than doubled the effective load capacity of an otherwise identical part. More broadly, running a project end to end on my own printer made it clear how much print orientation matters as a real design variable in FDM, not something to leave to slicer defaults. Going forward, I plan to use this same simulation-to-hardware loop on more involved parts to keep building intuition for where FEA predictions hold up and where real-world behavior diverges.
